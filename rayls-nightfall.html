Could Rayls or Nightfall carry private interbank payments at retail volume?

The requirement: private bank-to-bank payments, each settled individually with no batching, with Solidity programmability, and no single key or enclave whose compromise exposes all history. Rayls and Nightfall_4 are the two products closest to that shape, so we looked at both in depth.

Not measured: running Rayls's stack locally was blocked by this session's permission settings. Everything here comes from reading the open-source code and vendor documentation, plus public reports. estimate marks figures derived from those sources.

The short answer

BatchedRoughly 700–1,200/s, but payments wait for and settle with their batch.
Blast radiusThe operator can decrypt every payment. Every bank's viewing key is stored on the hub chain, encrypted to the operator's key.

Nightfall_4 (EY)

Chain costA genuine shielded pool with rollup proofs: about 6k gas per transfer, so the chain is no longer the limit.
ProposerOne proposer at a time, each proof needing 144 cores and 750 GB of RAM, and blocks of 64 that wait up to 120 s to fill. Proving time isn't published.
ProgrammableNo Solidity over private state, only transfers and token-for-token swaps. EY calls it experimental.
Bottom lineNeither meets the requirement. Rayls gets its throughput by batching and gives its operator the ability to read everything. Nightfall moves the bottleneck from the chain to a single, very large rollup prover.

Why these two

Solidity can't read a shielded note it doesn't own, so private programmability has three routes: run the logic where the plaintext already is, among the stakeholders; compute over ciphertexts with FHE; or use a TEE. FHE needs a network-wide decryption key, and TEEs are ruled out, so both have the all-history blast radius. That leaves the first route. Paladin and Rayls follow it; Nightfall is the best-known enterprise shielded pool.

Rayls: how a payment between banks works

  • Inside a bank: each bank runs a Privacy Node, its own EVM chain on axyl (Reth execution, Narwhal/Bullshark consensus). Any Solidity runs there. Rayls's “10k+ TPS” claim refers to this chain, so it covers transfers inside one bank.
  • Between banks: cash moves with Enygma. The sending bank's relayer collects its customers' outgoing transfers, proves them as one batch with one Groth16 proof, and submits it to the Private Network Hub.
  • The hub runs Hyperledger Besu with QBFT and ~5 s blocks, the same client we saturated at ~100–115 Mgas/s. It stores an encrypted balance per bank per token, not individual payments.
  • On arrival: the receiving bank's relayer decrypts the batch details and credits its customers on its own chain.

Rayls: throughput between banks

Each bank can submit only one batch per token per hub block. The proof's nullifier is Poseidon(bank secret, hub block number), and the hub contract rejects a reused nullifier. The relayer runs one cycle per token: fetch, prove, submit, wait, finalize. By default it handles 2 tokens at a time.

  • Batches aren't netted: each moves one bank's outgoing total to each destination bank. But the hub only ever sees per-bank totals, and every payment in a batch settles at the same moment. That is settlement batching, and it's how Rayls gets its throughput.
  • Sharding cash over several token contracts multiplies both rows, as it does for Zeto.
  • Brazil's central bank Drex pilot (phase 1) found the privacy solutions it tested, Rayls included, kept transactions anonymous but limited what authorities could see. The Drex platform as a whole reached up to 125 TPS, which the central bank warned may not suffice for a national system. We found no Rayls-specific TPS figure from the central bank.

Rayls: blast radius

The operator can decrypt everything, by design Each bank's viewing key is encrypted to the network operator's ML-KEM key and stored on the hub chain. At startup, the operator's Governance Listener recovers every bank's key, derives every bank pair's shared secret, and decrypts every payment's details, which are also stored on the hub chain. One operator key therefore unlocks all history: the same class of risk as a TEE.
  • ML-KEM is post-quantum: it protects against a future quantum computer decrypting stored data, not against the key being stolen.
  • Bank keys can be rotated. We found no threshold scheme or rotation for the operator's key.
  • No forward secrecy between banks: each pair agrees one shared secret and reuses it for every message.
  • Solidity between banks is asynchronous. A transfer can trigger up to 5 calls on the destination chain after it arrives. Atomic DvP goes through a separate ZkDvP note pool on the hub, at ~400k gas and ~10 s per swap (Rayls's figures).

Nightfall_4: yes, a shielded pool

EY's open-source project (CC0): a pool of note commitments and nullifiers, with a UltraPlonk proof from each client and a ZK rollup on top.

  • The chain problem is solved. A proposer collects a block of 64 transfers and proves the whole block once, so each transfer costs about 6,000 gas, against 949k for our Paladin payment. On the Besu chain we measured, that's ~16,000 transfers/s of capacity. This is the “proof-level batching” lever from the 2,000/s analysis.
  • The bottleneck moves to the proposer. Only one proposer is active at a time, and each block proof needs 144 cores and 750 GB of RAM. Proving time isn't published, and the proposer can't run on a laptop. Reaching 2,000/s would mean proving a 64-transfer block every 32 ms.
  • Payments wait for a block: assembly waits up to 120 s for a block to fill (first check after 15 s, target 25% full), which is batching in practice.
  • Clients prove one transaction at a time, taking “some seconds” each, so a bank would need many client instances to send in parallel.
  • Not programmable: deposit, transfer, withdraw, and an atomic two-party swap of different tokens (so DvP works), but no Solidity logic over private state.
  • Access is gated by X.509 certificates, with a sanctions-list check.
  • Maturity: its README says it is experimental and shouldn't be used for significant-value transactions, and EY disclaims ownership and liability.

What measuring Rayls would take

The stack runs fully locally and needs no testnet funds:

  1. Build rayls-sovereign-cli (Go) from source.
  2. Run rayls init --full --members 4 --no-blockscout. It pulls about a dozen public images from Rayls's AWS registry: hub, relayers, proof API, bank nodes and governance.
  3. Send crossTransfer() at increasing rates with batching effectively off, then on. Record per-payment latency, throughput, proof time per k, and hub gas per batch.
