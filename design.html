Recommended design · 5 November 2026

Private Solidity for interbank payments at retail volume

Keep Paladin at every bank, and keep Solidity. Change the shared ledger underneath so it checks payment proofs on all its cores at once, before putting payments in order. Then the only step done one at a time is a microsecond “is this note already spent?” test. Each payment settles on its own and is final in about a second.

Tags: measured on this laptop or in the PoC; estimate derived from measured costs, or from published figures, as cited.

  • The shared ledger takes two kinds of transaction in one stream.

Polymesh, in more detail

Polymesh is a blockchain for regulated securities. Its validators have to be licensed and approved through its own governance, and identity and compliance are built in. Two recent changes make it look close to what we want:

  • Solidity, since v8 (mainnet, 22 July 2026). Contracts compiled with the standard Solidity compiler run with full EVM compatibility. Polymesh says letting contracts use its built-in identity, compliance and settlement features is still in development.
  • Confidential Assets, which work like a shielded pool. The chain holds sealed account states and publishes a spent marker on each spend, so validators can't tell which account changed. Each transfer's details are stored on-chain, encrypted for the sender, receiver, any mediator and any auditor. Polymath's May 2026 press release called this launched; Polymesh's own documentation says it's live on testnet only, and not yet on mainnet.

Why it doesn't fit, today:

  • Not usable yet: the privacy feature isn't on mainnet, and Solidity can't call it. So there are no programmable private payments today.
  • The wrong shape for instant payments: settlement is an instruction that each party, and optionally a mediator, affirms before it executes. That suits securities, not a one-shot payment.
  • Blast radius: a regulated cash asset would have an auditor, and that one key opens every payment's details across all history. It's the same class of risk as Rayls's operator key, scoped per asset. Because the encrypted details stay on-chain for good, they're also exposed to future quantum decryption.
  • Throughput unknown: Polymesh publishes no throughput figures. Like all Substrate chains, its runtime applies transactions one at a time, so proof checks would be serial inside each block. That's the same structural limit we measured on Besu (my inference; not measured).

Verdict: worth watching, as the only product that combines Solidity, a shielded state model and bank-grade compliance. Look again when Confidential Assets reach mainnet and Solidity can drive them. Ask Polymesh for throughput figures, and for an audit model without a standing key that can read everything.

Why Besu tops out, and why an L2 doesn't help

A ledger node does three jobs for each transaction: it checks it (signatures and proofs), orders it (consensus), and applies it (updates the balances). Besu does all three in one loop, one block at a time, one transaction at a time. The proof check is the expensive part, but it depends on no shared state, so it could run on any core.

With 4 validators, the Besu figures drop to 259, 600 and 1,724. Two conclusions:

  • Checking proofs in parallel is easy and fast.
  • Even then, Besu's block pipeline can't pass about 2,000 transactions/s here. So the fix has to change the ledger's pipeline, not just the way proofs are checked.

“An L2 over a verification ledger is still throttled by the verification ledger.” That's right, and this design doesn't use one. A ledger can throttle payments in two ways:

  1. By doing serial work for each one. That's Besu.
  2. By making each one wait for finality somewhere else. That's a rollup: payments are final only when the outer chain accepts the batch, unless you trust a single sequencer.

This design fixes the first by making the ledger itself parallel. It avoids the second because each payment is final on this ledger. An optional anchor to Besu or Ethereum receives one state root every few seconds, after payments are already final, so it can't throttle anything.

The L2 options we rejected:

  • ZK rollups: final only once the batch proof lands.
  • Validiums such as Prividium: the operator sees everything.
  • Nightfall: one very large prover.
  • Optimistic rollups: days of challenge period.

Walkthrough: one payment, end to end

Alice banks with Bank A, and Bob banks with Bank B. Alice pays Bob €50. As with SEPA Instant or UPI today, Alice's and Bob's accounts stay in their banks' own systems. The shared ledger moves €50 of settlement money from Bank A to Bank B, and each bank holds that money as a pool of notes in its Paladin wallet. Times are cumulative and approximate.

What if…

  • …Bank A spends the €30 note twice, by bug or by fraud? Both transactions may pass the check on arrival, but the commit order is the same everywhere. The first one applies; the second fails the spent-marker test on every validator. There's no fork, and nothing to reconcile.
  • …the proof is wrong? It's dropped at step 5 and never ordered.
  • …validators fail? Up to 2 of 7 can crash or misbehave and the rest carry on: a BFT committee of 3f+1 tolerates f faulty validators.
  • …Bank B's node is offline? The payment is still final at step 8. Bank A's node keeps re-sending the note details until Bank B acknowledges them, and until then Bank B can't spend that €50. SEPA Instant checks that the payee's bank is reachable first; Bank A can do the same before step 4.

The same payment with a side effect

Say Bank A is paying for a bond Bank B delivers. Before step 3, both banks' nodes run the private Solidity contract (below) in their shared privacy group, and Bank B signs the group's new state. Bank A's proof covers the cash leg, tied to that state change. Bank A then submits both as one bundle, and at step 8 the ledger applies both or neither. That adds about 50–150 ms estimate.

How the fast consensus works, and where Solidity fits

Consensus has one job: making every validator end up with the same transactions in the same order, even if some validators crash or lie. It doesn't run Solidity or check proofs. The node does those before and after.

Why Besu's consensus can't go faster

  • How QBFT works: Besu uses QBFT, a leader-based protocol. Each round, one validator (the leader) builds a block and executes it. The others execute it too, then vote on it in two rounds. Only then does the next round start.
  • Why that limits it: execution sits inside every round, so a full block takes longer than its target. At saturation, our 1-second blocks stretched to 1.5–2 s. And every transaction goes through one leader at a time.

What a DAG-based consensus does differently

Leader-based (Besu's QBFT)DAG-based (Mysticeti, Narwhal/Bullshark, Hedera's hashgraph)
Who proposes transactionsOne leader per roundEvery validator, every round, in parallel
How votes happenSeparate vote messages, in two roundsBuilt in: each new block references the blocks its author has seen, and those references count as votes
When transactions executeBefore voting, inside every roundAfter ordering, outside the consensus loop
What limits itThe leader, and execution time per blockNetwork bandwidth across all validators
Numbers1,724–2,247 plain transfers/s here measuredMysticeti: over 200,000 transactions/s at about 0.5 s in published benchmarks; on Sui mainnet since 2024

A DAG (“directed acyclic graph”) is just the web of blocks pointing back at earlier blocks. Every validator sees the same web, so each can work out on its own, by a fixed rule, which blocks are committed and in what order. No validator waits for a leader, and there's no extra round of voting.

One ledger, not two chains

The two lanes aren't two chains. There's one set of validators, one order, one state and one state root, so there's no bridge. A transaction's lane matters only before ordering (what to check) and after it (how to apply it):

 1 ARRIVE      A bank sends a transaction to a validator.
               Payment: the validator checks its proof right away, on a spare core.
               Solidity transaction: the usual signature check.
 2 SHARE       Every ~100 ms, each validator publishes a small block: its new transactions,
               plus references to the blocks it has received from the others.
 3 AGREE       When enough later blocks reference a round's leader block, every validator
               independently concludes it's committed. No separate vote is needed.
 4 ORDER       That block, and everything it references that isn't yet committed, becomes
               one ordered list. Every validator computes the identical list.
 5 APPLY       The executor walks the list in order:
                 payment      → are its spent markers new? Record them; add its notes   (µs)
                 Solidity tx  → run it in the EVM, exactly as on Besu today             (ms, capped)
 6 CHECKPOINT  Validators sign the resulting state root. To Paladin this is an ordinary
               Ethereum block: the same JSON-RPC, the same events and receipts.

Only step 5 is done one at a time, and for a payment it takes a fraction of a millisecond. EVM work in each commit is capped, so a heavy contract can delay the payments ordered after it by tens of milliseconds at most.

How existing systems join the two lanes

  • Hedera: Solidity calls the built-in token service through a system contract at address 0x167, inside the same transaction. This design copies that model.
  • Hyperliquid: HyperEVM contracts read the built-in exchange's state as it stood when the EVM block was built. Writes go through a CoreWriter contract and are processed afterwards, and the EVM transaction succeeds whether or not the action is accepted. That's too loose for atomic payments.

The Solidity programming model

Developers write ordinary Solidity and deploy it inside a privacy group. To move cash, the contract emits a call to the cash system contract, and the bank's node turns that into a zero-knowledge transfer bundled with the group's state change. This is the contract we built and measured in the private payment tab:

// Deployed inside the A⇄B privacy group: only Bank A's and Bank B's nodes ever execute it.
contract PrivateSettlement {
    address immutable seller;
    address immutable buyer;
    mapping(address => uint256) public bondOf;
    event PenteExternalCall(address indexed contractAddress, bytes encodedCall);

    function settle(bytes32 tradeId, uint256 units, address cash, bytes calldata cashTransfer) external {
        require(msg.sender == seller, "seller only");
        bondOf[seller] -= units;                       // private side effect
        bondOf[buyer]  += units;
        emit PenteExternalCall(cash, cashTransfer);    // the cash leg, settled atomically with the above
    }
}
  • Programmable payments follow the same pattern, with different conditions before the cash call:
    • an oracle-signed delivery confirmation;
    • escrow released on a deadline;
    • coupon schedules;
    • netting logic between two banks, with each resulting payment still settled individually.
  • Rules that must hold for every payment, such as both parties being registered members or a per-payment limit, go into the cash proof. The ledger can't accept a payment that breaks them.
  • Simpler than on Besu: a payment with a side effect becomes one ledger transaction, with one proof and two signatures. On Besu it took 3 transactions, 2 proofs and 949k gas.

  • Hiero, Hedera's codebase, open source under Apache 2.0 at LF Decentralized Trust, the same foundation as Besu and Paladin.

Why you haven't heard of a “two-lane” architecture

Because “two lanes” is my label. The usual names are “native services” or “system contracts beside an EVM”, and “fast path versus consensus path”. The pattern is already in production:

  • Hedera: a built-in token service beside Besu's EVM, under one hashgraph consensus. Solidity calls the tokens through 0x167.
  • Hyperliquid: its built-in exchange (HyperCore) beside HyperEVM, under one consensus (HyperBFT).
  • Sui: a fast path for transactions that touch only their sender's objects, and full consensus for shared ones.
  • Ethereum itself: precompiles are native code for exactly the BN254 operations we measured. They just run one transaction at a time, inside each block.

The core idea is published central-bank research. Check every transaction in parallel first, and keep only a tiny “is it already spent?” step serial. That's the main result of Project Hamilton (Boston Fed and MIT, 2022): 1.7 million transactions/s, 99% complete in under a second. Like a note pool, Hamilton's processor stored only 32-byte hashes of unspent funds.

Why nobody has combined it with shielded notes and Solidity for banks:

  1. Privacy projects target public Ethereum, where the base layer can't be changed. So they're built as contracts (Zeto, Railgun) or L2s (Aztec, Nightfall), and inherit the EVM's or the L1's limits. That's what we measured.
  2. Vendors selling to bank consortia ship on unmodified clients, such as Paladin on Besu. Banks want a supported, audited client, and forking the ledger is a big commitment.
  3. The retail-scale research didn't use a shared ledger. Hamilton was a central processor, with no zero-knowledge; privacy-preserving auditing was left for future work. Shared-ledger projects with privacy have mostly targeted wholesale volumes.
  4. Two of the pieces are recent: fast native proof libraries (gnark, which Besu now ships) and DAG-based consensus in production (Mysticeti on Sui since 2024).

So it's new as a combination, not as an idea. That cuts both ways. There's no exotic research risk, but nobody has done the integration, so there's no reference deployment to copy.

SettlementNeverEach payment is its own transaction, gross and individually final at commit. No netting, and no waiting for others.
Consensus blocksYes, as in every ledgerA validator's block closes every ~100 ms, and a payment waits for at most one. It's the same as Besu blocks today, but faster.
Proof checkingOptionalChecking a block's proofs together is a mathematical shortcut that involves no waiting and roughly halves the cost. It isn't assumed in the numbers above.
External anchoringYesOne root per interval, after payments are already final. Payments never wait for it.

For comparison: a TEE, Rayls's operator key, or today's RTGS databaseAll of history
  • Regulator access without a master key:
    • Each bank encrypts an audit record of each payment to a per-period key, split k-of-n, for example among the central bank, the supervisor and a court.
    • Keys are destroyed after the retention period.
    • The records live with the regulator, not on the shared ledger.
  • Anonymity: unlike Rayls's 6-bank sets, notes hide which banks transacted from everyone but the two parties. Timing remains visible.
  • Submission metadata: the validator that receives a payment sees which bank's network address sent it. A shared relay, or always submitting through the central bank's validator, confines that to one party.

Risks

  • Invisible counterfeiting: a circuit bug in a shielded pool could mint money that nobody can see. The mitigations are:
    • formal verification of the circuits, as EthSystems did for its shielded pool in October 2026;
    • a supply turnstile that reconciles total supply against reserves on the EVM lane.
  • State growth: at 10,000 payments/s around the clock, the ledger gains about 1.7 billion notes and 1.7 billion spent markers a day. That's at least ~110 GB a day estimate. A shielded ledger can't delete spent notes, because it can't tell which they are. The plan is epochs:
    • start a fresh note tree and spent-marker set each period;
    • banks move their unspent notes across, a few thousand per bank;
    • archive the old tree and set.
  • First-of-a-kind integration: there's no reference deployment. The consensus is proven; the way the pieces join isn't.
  • Note management: a bank can send in parallel only as far as it holds spendable notes, so wallets must keep thousands of notes and pick them without conflicts. That's engineering, not a protocol limit.
  • Hot private groups: a group's state changes one step at a time. Split busy bank pairs by business line, or keep state per trade.
  • Proving cost lands on the banks. Size it with GPU provers before committing.
